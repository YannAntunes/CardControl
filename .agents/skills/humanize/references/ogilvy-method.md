# The Ogilvy method (extended reference)

In September 1982, David Ogilvy sent a one-page memo titled "How to Write" to everyone at Ogilvy & Mather. It listed ten rules. Its opening argument is the reason it's in this skill: woolly thinking produces woolly writing, and good writing is learned rather than inherited.

That claim matters here. If bad writing is a symptom of unclear thinking, then editing the words alone is treating the symptom. The skill's job is to name where the thinking hasn't landed, not just to swap out the vocabulary.

The rules are paraphrased below, not reproduced. Source: Ogilvy's 1982 internal memo, via Nicolas Cole's transcription and commentary (July 2026).

---

## The five the skill can check

These are inspectable in a text, so `SKILL.md` runs them on every draft.

### 1. Write the way you talk

Ogilvy's instruction is to write naturally, in the voice you already use.

The practical read: nobody needs to go find a writing voice, because they have one, and most bad business writing comes from abandoning it for something that sounds more official. Cole's method for getting at it is to record yourself talking about the topic and transcribe that as your first draft.

For this skill, the rule cuts two ways. It's the strongest argument for **preserve mode** (the writer's spoken rhythm is the asset). It's also the test to apply in either mode: read it aloud, and fix wherever you stumble.

### 2. Short words, short sentences, short paragraphs

The most-quoted rule and the easiest to over-apply.

Ogilvy's test is aural, not numeric. A 40-word sentence that reads aloud cleanly is fine. A 20-word one that makes you backtrack is not. Use length as a trigger to look, never as a limit to enforce, and don't chop a writer's long spoken sentences into uniform short ones. That trades one robotic rhythm for another.

Latinate-to-plain swaps worth having ready: utilize/use, commence/start, sufficient/enough, additional/more, terminate/end, purchase/buy, demonstrate/show, endeavor/try, facilitate/help, indicate/show, obtain/get, require/need.

### 3. Never use jargon

Ogilvy's examples were invented abstractions like *reconceptualize* and *attitudinally*, and his verdict on people who use them was blunt.

The useful part is his diagnosis: jargon hides a lack of understanding. So the fix isn't a thesaurus lookup. Ask whether the writer can say the thing plainly. If they can, use the plain version. If they can't, the problem is upstream in the thinking, and saying so is more useful than any word swap.

Three families to flag:

- **Invented abstractions** — `-ize`, `-ization`, `-ality`, and adverbs built off nominalizations: operationalize, socialize (a doc), ideate, incentivize, directionally, functionality, actionability.
- **Corporate filler** — circle back, bandwidth, low-hanging fruit, move the needle, boil the ocean, double-click on, level-set, north star.
- **Unexplained domain terms** — fine for an expert audience, fatal for a general one. This is why the skill asks who the piece is for.

Cole's shortcut: write as if to an eighth grader. Useful for calibration, but don't let it strip real precision. Plain isn't the same as simplified.

### 4. Check your quotations

The shortest rule and the one with the highest stakes for an AI editor, because fabricating a plausible quote is exactly the failure mode language models have.

The skill's line: flag every quotation, statistic, date, and attribution as needing verification, state plainly that it can't verify them, and never invent, complete, or tidy a quote. A quote with no source gets flagged. A number with no unit or baseline gets flagged. These go in a **Needs your check** section that never gets buried.

### 5. Make the ask crystal clear

Before sending, the writer should be certain the recipient knows what to do next.

This is the check almost no draft passes and almost nobody runs. A buried ask is a critical finding in an email, not a stylistic nitpick. The test is whether a reader could state the next action after one pass. For social and longform, the equivalent is the takeaway: name it, or say the draft doesn't have one.

## The four that belong to the writer

A skill can't do these. It shouldn't pretend to, and it shouldn't stay silent about them either. `SKILL.md` surfaces the relevant one on a high-stakes draft, once.

### Read the Roman-Raphaelson book three times

Ogilvy's first rule points at *Writing That Works* by Kenneth Roman and Joel Raphaelson. It's the source text behind most of the rest of the memo. Recommend it; don't try to be it.

### Never send it the day you write it

Sleep on it, read it aloud the next morning, then edit. Ogilvy called this his rule for anything that matters, and Cole calls it the single best piece of writing advice he gives.

An AI editor cannot give a draft time. It can return the draft immediately and tell the writer to sit on it, which is the honest version of the rule. Worth saying on anything important, because the speed of getting an edit back is exactly what tempts people to skip the wait.

### Get a colleague to improve it

For important pieces, add a human reader after the overnight gap. This skill is not that reader. It catches patterns; a colleague catches whether the argument lands with someone who has to act on it.

### If you want action, don't write

The last rule is the most contrarian and the most useful: writing is a poor substitute for going and asking. If the draft exists to make one person do one thing, and a direct conversation is possible, the best edit is not sending it.

A writing skill recommending you not write is a feature. Say it when it applies.

## On the two-page rule

Ogilvy capped any subject at two pages. Taken literally it conflicts with longform work, so this skill applies the reasoning instead of the limit: if it can't fit in two pages, the thinking usually hasn't been simplified yet.

In practice that means flagging bloat and naming what could go, then leaving the decision to the writer. Don't silently truncate anyone's essay to honor a memo rule about memos.

## Where this sits next to the AI-slop pass

The two passes catch different failures, and both are needed.

`references/ai-slop-tells.md` catches writing that sounds **machine-made**: binary contrasts, puffery, colon reveals, synonym cycling. Post-2022 problems.

This file catches writing that is **bad**, which is a much older problem: fog, jargon, buried asks, unverified quotes. A draft can pass every AI-slop check and still fail here, and that draft is worse than one with a stray em dash in it.

Slop removal is hygiene. This is the craft.
