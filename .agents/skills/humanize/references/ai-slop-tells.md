# AI-slop tell bank (extended reference)

Distilled from Wikipedia "Signs of AI writing", academic word-frequency studies, editor and copywriter sources, `blader/humanizer`, and `Tradelord223/plain-english`. Use this reference when the checklist is not enough or when the user asks why a pattern is weak.

**Golden rule:** no single tell is proof. One em dash is fine. A pile of tells together is the signal. Edit for the pile, not the individual.

---

## 1. Lexical tells (overused words)

These spiked in published text after late 2022. When several co-occur in one passage, that's the flag.

**Verbs:** delve, leverage, utilize, foster, garner, underscore, showcase, highlight, emphasize, enhance, elevate, embark, harness, unlock, navigate, boast, exemplify, bolster, cultivate, resonate, align, streamline, spearhead.

**Adjectives:** crucial, pivotal, vital, key, robust, comprehensive, seamless, vibrant, meticulous, intricate, multifaceted, profound, enduring, groundbreaking, renowned, innovative, dynamic, rich, diverse, holistic, nuanced, bespoke.

**Nouns:** landscape, tapestry, realm, testament, beacon, journey, interplay, synergy, ecosystem, framework, paradigm, cornerstone, hub, array, treasure trove.

**Transitions / connectives:** moreover, furthermore, additionally, notably, importantly, ultimately, consequently, thus, hence.

**Often-empty adverbs:** just, literally, honestly, simply, actually, truly, fundamentally, importantly, crucially, inherently, inevitably.

The point isn't to ban every one of these. It's that AI reaches for them by default. A human picks the plain word.

The adverbs need a lighter hand than the rest. "Honestly, I have no idea" and "it literally doubled" are doing work. Cut them where they pad, keep them where they carry emphasis, contrast, real uncertainty, or the writer's spoken rhythm. Stripping every one of them is a classic over-correction that leaves prose stiffer than it started.

## 2. Phrase & cliché tells

**Significance puffery:** "stands as a testament to", "serves as a reminder", "plays a vital/crucial/pivotal role", "underscores the importance of", "leaves an indelible mark", "marks a turning point", "in the ever-evolving landscape of", "deeply rooted in", "setting the stage for".

**Opening filler:** "In today's fast-paced world", "In the digital age", "Now more than ever", "When it comes to X", "Let's dive in", "Let's explore", "Picture this".

**Hedge filler:** "It's worth noting that", "It's important to note", "It's important to remember", "Needless to say", "That said".

**Vague authority:** "Experts argue", "Studies show", "Observers have noted", "Industry reports suggest", "It is widely believed", "Many would agree" — with no actual source.

**Geographic / descriptive cliché:** "nestled in the heart of", "boasts a rich cultural heritage", "a vibrant tapestry of", "breathtaking natural beauty", "hidden gem".

**Closers:** "In conclusion", "In summary", "At the end of the day", "The bottom line is", "Only time will tell", "The future looks bright", "What are your thoughts?", "The possibilities are endless".

## 3. Structural tells

- **The "not X, but Y" reflex** — "It's not just a tool, it's a revolution." / "This isn't about speed; it's about trust." / "No fluff, no filler, just results." The single most recognizable AI sentence shape. Kill on sight.
- **Compulsive rule of three** — three adjectives, three clauses, three bullet points, everywhere. Real writing varies the count.
- **Topic-sentence → support → restate** in every paragraph. Same shape repeated. Break it: open with the detail or the conclusion sometimes.
- **Uniform paragraph and sentence length.** AI writes even blocks. Humans burst: a long sentence, then three words.
- **Listicle bloat** — turning prose into bullets that all start with a bold label and a colon. **Speed:** … **Quality:** … **Trust:** …
- **Header overload** — an H2 every 80 words for a short piece.
- **Present-participle significance tails** — "…, creating a sense of community." "…, highlighting its lasting impact." "…, ensuring success." Cut the comma-plus-ing tail; it adds nothing.
- **Fake balance** — "On one hand… on the other hand…" with no actual stance taken.
- **Formulaic conclusion** — "Despite its challenges, X continues to thrive." Restates the intro and commits to nothing.
- **Negative listing** — "Not a framework. Not a library. A compiler." A cousin of "not X, but Y". Say the last one and move on.
- **Throat-clearing openers** — "Here's the thing", "Let me be clear", "I'll be honest", "The uncomfortable truth is". They announce that a point is coming instead of making it.
- **Faux-insight setups** — "What nobody tells you", "the part everyone misses", "what most people get wrong". These flatter the writer as the lone expert who sees through it. Cut the setup and let the claim carry itself. If the claim can't survive without the frame, it wasn't insight.
- **Colon reveals** — a noun phrase, a colon, then a lowercase dramatic reveal. "The detail that makes it work: a separate agent grades it." "The best part: it learns." Colons are for lists, labels, and quotes. Using them for suspense is a tic, and it stacks fast: three in a row is unmistakable.
- **Fake-strong verbs** — "serves as a centralized hub for", "acts as a bridge between", "plays host to". These inflate a plain relationship. "Is" and "has" are often the honest verbs, and a real specific verb ("tracks", "routes", "blocks") is better than both.
- **Synonym cycling (elegant variation)** — rotating terms for the same thing so a word doesn't repeat: the agent, then the assistant, then the tool, then the system. Reads as vocabulary anxiety. If the clear word is right, repeat it. Repetition of the correct noun is invisible to readers; rotation is not.
- **Fake-profound kickers** — the closing line that turns the argument into a metaphor or aphorism. "The tools change. The craft doesn't." The right fix is deletion, not a better metaphor. Rewriting the kicker keeps the shape that made it slop; end on the clearest concrete sentence the draft already has.
- **Rhetorical setups** — "What if I told you", "Think about it:", "Plot twist:", and self-answered "Question? Answer." pairs.
- **False ranges** — "from X to Y" when X and Y do not form a scale, sequence, or real span. Replace the range with a direct list.
- **Formulaic challenge and outlook sections** — a stock problem paragraph followed by vague optimism. Keep sourced problems or plans. Cut the frame.
- **Heading echoes** — a heading followed by a first sentence that restates the heading before the text gives a new fact.
- **Diff-anchored prose** — documentation or comments that narrate what the old version did. Describe current behavior unless the genre is about change.
- **Unraised objections** — "I'm not saying", "don't get me wrong", or "you might think" when no reader or source raised the objection. Keep real objections that the text attributes and answers.
- **Fake alternatives** — an unlikely option introduced only so the draft can reject it. State the actual constraint instead.

## 4. Punctuation & formatting tells

- **Em dash overuse (—).** Banned by default in this skill (`--keep-em-dash` overrides). Replace with a period, comma, colon, or parentheses. A real interjection can use parentheses or a comma.
- **Boldface scattered through prose** to mark "key terms" that don't need marking.
- **Title Case In Every Heading.** Use sentence case.
- **Emoji as bullet points** or section markers.
- **Curly-quote / straight-quote inconsistency** copied from a model.
- **Every list mechanically Oxford-comma'd and perfectly parallel** — too clean to be human.

## 5. Tone tells

- **Relentless positivity.** No friction, no downside, everything is exciting and transformative.
- **No real opinion.** Surveys both sides, commits to neither. Humanized writing takes a stance.
- **Over-explaining.** Defines the obvious, repeats the point, adds a recap nobody asked for.
- **Marketing voice on a non-marketing task** — "Elevate your workflow", "Unlock the power of", "Take it to the next level".
- **Abstraction over specifics.** "Drives meaningful results" instead of "cut load time from 4s to 800ms". Concrete nouns and real numbers are the strongest human signal.
- **Engagement bait** — rhetorical question closers, "Let me know in the comments", manufactured urgency.
- **Chatbot wrappers** — "Certainly", "Great question", "I hope this helps", process narration, or an offer to continue inside a standalone deliverable.
- **Knowledge-limit padding** — cutoff dates, "details are scarce", and "based on available information" used before a guess. State only what the source supports.
- **Stacked qualifiers** — several caveats that repair an earlier overstatement. Keep the smallest qualifier that preserves the source's uncertainty.

## 6. Positive humanizing moves (what to do instead)

- **Vary sentence length deliberately.** Burstiness reads human. Follow a long sentence with a short one. Or a fragment.

  There's a real tension here with "dramatic fragmentation" above, and it's worth stating plainly: varied rhythm is good, stacked punchy fragments are bad. The test is whether the short sentence carries meaning or just beats a drum. "It shipped Tuesday." carries. "That's it. That's the whole thing." beats a drum. When editing someone else's writing, lean toward leaving their rhythm alone.
- **Lead with the concrete.** Numbers, names, dates, specific objects. Cut the abstract framing sentence in front of them.
- **Take a position.** Say which option is better and why. Cut the hedges that walk it back.
- **Use contractions** where natural (don't, can't, it's). Stiff = robotic.
- **Cut the scaffolding.** Delete the intro that announces what you'll say and the conclusion that recaps it. Start at the first real sentence; end at the last real one.
- **Plain words beat fancy ones.** "use" not "utilize", "show" not "showcase", "help" not "foster", "deep" not "profound".
- **Active voice, real subject.** "The team shipped it" beats "It was shipped by the team".
- **Let one word repeat** if it's the right word. AI swaps synonyms to avoid repetition (elegant variation); humans don't bother.

## False positives — do not over-correct

- A single em dash, one rule-of-three, or one contraction is not slop. Context decides.
- Technical and legal writing is *meant* to be plain and repetitive. Don't inject personality there.
- Code identifiers, commands, paths, types, error text, product names, and terms of art must stay exact. Define an unfamiliar term once when the audience needs it.
- Some flagged words are sometimes the right word ("crucial" when something is actually crucial). Judgement over keyword-matching.
- The goal is writing that reads like a sharp human wrote it, not text engineered to beat an AI detector.
